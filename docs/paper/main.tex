Atomistic Materials Discovery on Leadership-Class High-Performance Computing}

\author{
\IEEEauthorblockN{Massimiliano Lupo Pasini$^{1}$}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Oak Ridge National Laboratory}\\
Oak Ridge, TN, USA\\
lulopasim@ornl.gov}
}

\begin{abstract}
We present \textsc{matsim-agents}, an agentic \ac{AI} workflow framework for
atomistic materials discovery on leadership-class \ac{HPC} systems. The framework connects natural-language scientific objectives to
executable simulation tasks, \ac{MLIP}-based relaxation, uncertainty-aware
validation, selective first-principles escalation, and scientific summarization.
Its key novelty is a closed-loop, \acs{HPC}-deployable workflow in which uncertainty
estimates act as control variables that route predictions either to analysis or
to \ac{DFT}-driven active learning. The framework supports user-selectable \ac{MLIP} backends---HydraGNN for
exascale-pretrained graph-neural-network potentials, UMA
for universal foundation-model potentials, and the MACE-MP foundation
models---and user-selectable \ac{DFT} backends,
including open-source \ac{QE} and licensed closed-source \ac{VASP}, with deployment
support for heterogeneous \ac{DOE} leadership-class systems. Rather than promoting
any single potential, the framework treats the \ac{MLIP} as an interchangeable,
benchmarkable component: the same cold-start vs.\ warm-start instrument that
accelerates \ac{DFT} also measures how each backend behaves for a given
material, enabling users to switch and benchmark backends against one another,
combine them as a mixture of experts, or seed active-learning data collection
from any of them---without changing the orchestration or deployment layers. We demonstrate the complete loop running unattended on
\ac{NERSC} Perlmutter---from \ac{MLIP}-driven sampling through concurrent
\ac{DFT} labeling to per-case model retraining---and use the resulting
active-learning-corrected data to benchmark fine-tuning across all three
\ac{MLIP} backends under a single like-for-like protocol. In its current
capabilities, the active-learning loop is exercised specifically to estimate the
chemical and dynamical stability of an ensemble of candidate atomistic
structures; the framework therefore automates materials \emph{characterization},
an essential prerequisite for informed materials discovery and design. To couple
these measured accuracies to scientific decisions, an optional multi-\acs{LLM}
hypothesis-debate stage---an \acs{LLM} proposer with a panel of independent
critics reasoning only over the reported surrogate errors---returns materially
different crystal-structure stability verdicts before versus after fine-tuning
across two high-entropy alloys and a LiFePO$_4$ cathode: it escalates to
\acs{DFT} when the potential cannot resolve the tens-of-meV/atom spacing between
competing polymorphs, asserts the stable structure under an uncertainty gate once
fine-tuning closes that gap, and withholds claims---while flagging cases where
fine-tuning degrades a backend---when the residual error remains too large.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Accelerating the discovery of novel functional materials remains one of the
grand challenges at the intersection of computational science and
\acs{HPC}. First-principles electronic-structure methods such
as \acs{DFT} provide accurate energies and forces but scale as $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ in
the number of electrons and are therefore prohibitively expensive for
high-throughput exploration of large chemical spaces. \Acp{MLIP}~\cite{behler2007,bartok2010,batzner2022} offer
near-\acs{DFT} accuracy at a fraction of the cost, but their reliability degrades
silently outside the training distribution, making undetected extrapolation a
practical risk in autonomous workflows.

Agentic \acs{AI} offers a principled route through autonomous planning, tool invocation,
conditional branching, and iterative self-improvement to address both challenges. By embedding an \ac{MLIP} inside a decision-making
loop equipped with \ac{UQ} and \acs{DFT} escalation, an
agentic workflow can \emph{explore broadly} with the \acs{MLIP} and \emph{correct
selectively} with \acs{DFT}, reserving expensive quantum-mechanical calculations for
only those configurations where the \acs{MLIP} is genuinely uncertain.

Existing active learning workflows for \acp{MLIP}~\cite{smith2018,vandermause2020,
podryabinkin2017} already automate the cycle of candidate generation, \acs{DFT}
labeling, and model retraining. \textsc{matsim-agents} advances this paradigm
by introducing a closed-loop agentic orchestration layer that connects
natural-language scientific objectives to executable atomistic simulation tasks,
\acs{MLIP}-based exploration, uncertainty-aware validation, selective \acs{DFT} escalation,
active learning, and language-level scientific synthesis. This loop transforms
materials simulation from a manually configured sequence of computational steps
into an autonomous scientific workflow in which planning, execution,
validation, correction, and reporting are coordinated by interacting agents.

The novelty of \textsc{matsim-agents} lies in making the full decision cycle
explicit and executable on leadership-class \acs{HPC} systems. A user can state an
open-ended materials objective in natural language; the planner converts that
objective into typed atomistic tasks; the executor performs fast \acs{MLIP}-based
relaxation through a user-selected backend (HydraGNN, UMA, or MACE-MP); the \acs{UQ} gate
decides whether the prediction is reliable enough for
analysis or must be escalated to \ac{DFT}-driven active learning; and the analyst
returns an interpretable scientific summary. In this design, uncertainty is not
an offline diagnostic but a first-class workflow control variable that determines
whether the agentic system accepts, corrects, or improves its own predictions.

In its current capabilities, the active-learning workflow is exercised
specifically to estimate the chemical and dynamical stability of an ensemble of
candidate atomistic structures---determining which crystallographic structures
are chemically and dynamically stable for a given set of elements and for varying
stoichiometric formulas. In this scope the framework automates materials
\emph{characterization}, which is a crucial prerequisite for informed materials
discovery and design rather than a substitute for it.

A second important capability is explicit user control over the first-principles
backend. The workflow supports both \ac{QE}~\cite{giannozzi2009qe,giannozzi2017qe}, an open-source \acs{DFT}
code, and \ac{VASP}~\cite{kresse1993vasp,kresse1996vasp}, a licensed closed-source \acs{DFT} code. The user can select the \acs{DFT}
engine most appropriate for their scientific problem, institutional software
access, pseudopotential library, and production workflow. To make this choice
practical on \acs{DOE} systems, \textsc{matsim-agents} provides detailed bash scripts
for compiling both \acs{QE} and \acs{VASP} on the three main \ac{DOE} leadership-class
supercomputing facilities: \ac{NERSC} Perlmutter, \ac{ALCF} Aurora, and \ac{OLCF} Frontier.

In this paper we present \textsc{matsim-agents}, an open-source framework that
realises this agentic materials-discovery loop. The main novel contributions of
this work are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{closed-loop agentic workflow} that translates natural-language
  materials objectives into executable atomistic simulation tasks through a
  checkpointed LangGraph multi-agent state machine, integrating \ac{LLM} planning,
  backend-agnostic \acs{MLIP} relaxation, uncertainty-gated validation, \acs{DFT} labeling,
  active learning, and scientific summarization in a single reproducible loop.
\item A \textbf{\acs{DFT} warm-start capability} in which \acs{MLIP}-relaxed structures are
  used to initialize higher-fidelity first-principles optimization, evaluated
  with a \emph{factorial backend ablation} that pairs each \acs{MLIP} backend
  (HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} and UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}) with each \acs{DFT}
  solver (\acs{QE} and \acs{VASP}). Beyond
  accelerating \acs{DFT} convergence, the cold-start vs.\ warm-start comparison serves
  two additional roles in the agentic workflow: it acts as a computational
  validation safety net for the \acs{MLIP} predictions by testing whether the
  \acs{MLIP}-relaxed geometry is genuinely closer to the \acs{DFT} minimum, and it
  exposes regions of \acs{MLIP} weakness in materials space---cases where warm-start
  benefit is absent or where \acs{DFT} convergence degrades---that can be used to
  target active learning data collection and improve model robustness.
\item A \textbf{closed-loop active-learning demonstration and cross-backend
  fine-tuning benchmark}: the full loop is run unattended end-to-end on
  Perlmutter across five materials cases---driving \acs{MLIP}-\acs{MD} sampling,
  \acs{MC}-dropout acquisition, and concurrent \acs{DFT} labeling with
  per-iteration checkpointing---and the resulting active-learning-corrected
  datasets are used to fine-tune and evaluate all three \acs{MLIP} backends
  (HydraGNN, UMA, and the MACE-MP capacities) under a single like-for-like
  protocol, so the loop both generates targeted training data and measures how
  much each potential improves on the chemistry it sampled.
\item An \textbf{\acs{HPC}-oriented deployment model} that supports local \acs{LLM}
  inference, \acs{GPU}-accelerated atomistic simulation, batch-scheduled
  first-principles calculations, checkpointed workflow execution, and
  platform-specific support for heterogeneous \ac{DOE} leadership-class systems.
\end{itemize}

The paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related
work. Section~\ref{sec:architecture} describes the system architecture.
Section~\ref{sec:hpc} details the \acs{HPC} deployment strategy.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents benchmark results on Perlmutter.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} discusses findings, limitations, and future
developments, and draws conclusions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Machine-Learned Interatomic Potentials}
The past decade has seen rapid development of \acp{MLIP}, from kernel-based
Gaussian approximation potentials~\cite{bartok2010} and behler-Parrinello
networks~\cite{behler2007} to graph-neural-network architectures such as
NequIP~\cite{batzner2022}, MACE~\cite{batatia2022}, and
SchNet~\cite{schutt2018}. Foundation models trained on diverse datasets
(such as CHGNet~\cite{deng2023}, MACE-MP~\cite{batatia2023macemp}, and
UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}) have further broadened coverage. HydraGNN is an atomistic \ac{GFM} trained in a recent exascale multi-task
workflow, using shared message-passing representations with
dataset-/fidelity-specific prediction heads~\cite{hydragnn,lupopasini2026exascalegfm}.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification for MLIPs}
\ac{UQ} for \acp{MLIP} has been studied via deep ensembles~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017},
\ac{MC}-Dropout~\cite{gal2016}, and calibrated committee models~\cite{schran2020}.
Ensembles are the most reliable but require $N$ independent model evaluations.
In multi-branch atomistic models, branch-selection signals can also expose
information about how predictions are routed across dataset- or
fidelity-specialized heads; \textsc{matsim-agents} repurposes the HydraGNN
BranchWeightMLP router in exactly this way, as detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:architecture}. Such branch weights should be
interpreted as routing signals rather than calibrated Bayesian uncertainty.

\subsection{Active Learning for Materials}
Active learning has been used to iteratively expand \acs{MLIP} training sets with
targeted \acs{DFT} calculations, and several mature frameworks now automate this
cycle. FLARE~\cite{vandermause2020,vandermause2022} uses Bayesian force fields
with on-the-fly uncertainty to trigger \acs{DFT} calls during \acs{MD}.
DP-GEN~\cite{zhang2020dpgen} implements a concurrent-learning scheme for
DeePMD potentials that alternates exploration, labeling, and training across
many chemical systems. FIRETASKS / Atomate~\cite{mathew2017atomate} provides
a workflow engine for high-throughput \acs{DFT} and can be combined with \acs{MLIP}
training loops. ATAT and ICET~\cite{icet2019} focus on cluster expansion
fitting with active selection of supercell configurations.
ALF~\cite{schran2020} demonstrated committee-based active learning for
aqueous systems. More recently, foundation-model-aware AL schemes such as
Finetuner~\cite{batatia2023macemp} fine-tune MACE-MP on targeted datasets.
These frameworks demonstrate that candidate generation, uncertainty-based
selection, first-principles labeling, and retraining can be automated into
iterative materials-discovery pipelines.

\subsection{LLM-Driven Scientific Workflows}
Recent work has explored \acp{LLM} for materials hypothesis generation~\cite{
merchant2023,xie2023,sprueill2023}, automated literature synthesis, and code
generation. These developments show the potential of language models to assist
scientific reasoning, tool use, and human-facing interpretation of complex
simulation outputs.

\subsection{DFT Warm-Starting}
Using a fast approximate model to generate a near-equilibrium input geometry
for a more expensive method is a classical idea in computational
chemistry~\cite{lejaeghere2016}. Several recent works have proposed \acs{MLIP}
warm-starts for \acs{DFT} geometry optimisation~\cite{schreiner2022,merchant2023}.
These efforts motivate the use of learned potentials as fast preprocessors for
higher-fidelity first-principles calculations.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Overview}
\textsc{matsim-agents} is structured around three layers:
(i) a \emph{natural language interface} layer accepting free-text objectives
or interactive chat; (ii) a \emph{LangGraph orchestration layer} implementing
the multi-agent graph; and (iii) an \emph{atomistic backend layer} providing
\acs{MLIP} relaxation, stability scoring, and \acs{DFT} execution. The remainder
of this section is organised around these layers. We first describe the
combined interface and orchestration layer, comprising the agent graph, its
nodes, and the three entry points that drive it; we then describe the atomistic
backend layer, comprising the pluggable \acs{MLIP} engine (HydraGNN by default,
with UMA and the MACE-MP foundation models also selectable), the BranchWeightMLP
confidence signal, the active learning loop, and the \acs{DFT} warm-start
protocol.

\subsection{Interface and Orchestration Layer}
The first two layers---the natural-language interface
and the LangGraph orchestration layer---are realized together by a single
multi-agent graph and the entry points that expose it to the user.

\subsubsection{Agent Graph and Nodes}
The agent graph is assembled with LangGraph~\cite{langgraph} and contains four
primary nodes:

\textbf{Planner.} A configurable \ac{LLM} (Qwen2.5-72B served via
vLLM~\cite{vllm} by default, but interchangeable with any of the supported
open- or closed-weight models discussed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusions})
receives the user objective and emits a typed list of
\texttt{TaskSpec} items (\texttt{relax}, \texttt{analyze}, \texttt{report}).
Structured output via Pydantic schemas is used to ensure deterministic \acs{JSON}
parsing.

\textbf{Executor.} For each pending \texttt{relax} task, the executor invokes
the \acs{MLIP} relaxation tool, which runs \ac{ASE} \acs{FIRE}/\acs{BFGS}
optimisation using a user-selected \acs{MLIP} backend---HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn}
(graph-neural-network \acs{GFM}), UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} (universal
foundation-model potential), or the MACE-MP foundation
models~\cite{batatia2022,batatia2023macemp}---as a
force-and-energy evaluator. Results
(optimised structure, final energy, max force, convergence flag, \acs{UQ} scores) are
appended to the shared typed state.

\textbf{\acs{UQ} Gate.} The gate is a \emph{post-relaxation routing step}: it
does not steer the relaxation itself---which is a standard \acs{FIRE}/\acs{BFGS}
minimisation---but instead evaluates the branch-weight distribution captured at
each already-relaxed structure. If the mean top-branch
weight exceeds a configurable threshold (default 0.6) and the fraction of
unreliable predictions is below a second threshold (default 0.25), the gate
passes control to the analyst. Otherwise, it triggers the active learning
handoff, logging the decision to a \ac{JSONL} audit file. This branch-weight
signal is specific to HydraGNN's composition-conditioned branch router (the
BranchWeightMLP); the foundation-model backends (UMA and MACE-MP) expose no
analogous per-structure routing weight, so the branch-weight gate applies only
to the HydraGNN backend.

\textbf{Analyst.} The analyst node invokes the \acs{LLM} to generate a natural
language summary of the discovered structures, including energy rankings,
stability estimates, and \acs{UQ} signals.

\subsubsection{Entry Points and Task Complexity}
\textsc{matsim-agents} exposes these nodes through three entry points that form
a ladder of increasing task complexity. The simplest is the \emph{default
linear graph} described above: a fixed planner~$\rightarrow$~executor~$\rightarrow$~\acs{UQ}
gate~$\rightarrow$~analyst pipeline that handles a single, well-posed objective
(for example, ``relax this structure and report its stability'') in one
forward pass, with the only branch being the binary \acs{UQ} decision to escalate
or summarize. This entry point is best suited to one-shot tasks over a small,
explicitly given set of structures.

The second entry point is the \emph{supervisor graph}, which targets more
complex objectives that require systematic exploration of a composition space
rather than a fixed structure list. It decomposes the workflow into five
explicitly supervised nodes (prepare, explore, evaluate\_uq, optionally active
learning, and summarize) and adds a supervisor that can iterate over many
candidate phases, loop back into active learning when the \acs{UQ} gate flags
unreliable regions, and aggregate results across the enumeration. The added
node granularity gives the orchestrator finer control over how exploration,
validation, and correction are interleaved for multi-structure campaigns.

The third and most open-ended entry point is \emph{discovery chat}, an
interactive \ac{REPL} for exploratory, human-in-the-loop investigation. On top
of the underlying graph it adds automatic composition detection from
free-text input, optional multi-\acs{LLM} peer review (a proposer/critic debate
that stress-tests candidate hypotheses), and slash commands (\texttt{/relax},
\texttt{/al}, \texttt{/clear}) that let the user invoke individual stages on
demand. This entry point trades the bounded, single-objective scope of the
other two for open-ended, iterative scientific dialogue in which the task is
refined interactively rather than fixed in advance.

\subsection{Atomistic Backend Layer}
The third layer executes the physics. It evaluates energies and forces with a
configuration-selected \acs{MLIP} backend---HydraGNN by default, with the UMA
and MACE-MP foundation models selectable through the same interface---derives a
low-cost confidence signal from the model's composition-conditioned branch
router, and escalates uncertain structures to \acs{DFT} through the active
learning and warm-start protocols. We describe the HydraGNN \ac{GFM} engine in
detail below as the default backend; the alternative foundation-model backends
plug into the identical calculator, acquisition, and labelling interfaces and
are chosen by a single configuration switch (\texttt{MLIP\_BACKEND}) without
changing the orchestration code, so the pretrained potential itself is an
interchangeable component rather than a hard-coded dependency.

\subsubsection{HydraGNN GFM as the MLIP Engine}
The default backend is the HydraGNN \ac{GFM}~\cite{hydragnn,lupopasini2026exascalegfm},
an exascale-pretrained, multi-fidelity atomistic model rather than a narrow
single-dataset force field. Its multi-task design---shared \ac{MPNN} layers over
16 dataset-/fidelity-specific output branches, trained on a multi-source
first-principles corpus of more than 544 million structures spanning over 85
elements---gives it broad chemical coverage, which matters here because the
planner can generate tasks over chemistries not enumerated in advance. The
compact PaiNN-based lead checkpoint ($\sim$12M parameters, \acs{FP64}) evaluates
energies and forces orders of magnitude faster than direct \acs{DFT} during
relaxation and screening, while its branch structure supplies the routing signal
used by the confidence gate below.

\subsubsection{BranchWeightMLP Confidence Signal}
The BranchWeightMLP was introduced in the HydraGNN \acs{GFM} to make the
multi-branch, multi-task architecture deployable on chemistries that may not
belong cleanly to a single training dataset~\cite{lupopasini2026exascalegfm}.
It maps a per-element chemical-composition descriptor to a softmax-normalized
weight vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$, with $\sum_k w_k = 1$,
that blends the predictions of the 16 output branches into a single energy and
force prediction.

\textsc{matsim-agents} reuses the same branch-weight distribution as an inexpensive
workflow-level confidence signal. The top-branch weight
$w_\text{top} = \max_k w_k$ and the Shannon entropy
$H = -\sum_k w_k \log w_k$ indicate how concentrated or diffuse the router is:

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/BranchMLP_UQ.png}
\caption{BranchWeightMLP-based uncertainty heuristic in \textsc{matsim-agents}.
A candidate structure's chemical-composition vector is mapped by the
BranchWeightMLP router to softmax weights over the 16 dataset-/fidelity-specialized
HydraGNN output branches. The same weights both fuse the per-branch energy/force
predictions and yield routing-concentration statistics that
drive the \acs{UQ} gate: sharp routing is accepted for analysis, whereas diffuse
routing triggers additional validation or \acs{DFT} labeling.}
\label{fig:branchuq}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item $w_\text{top} \approx 1$ and $H \approx 0$ indicate that the chemistry is
  routed primarily through one dataset-/fidelity-specialized branch.
\item Lower $w_\text{top}$ and larger $H$ indicate that the model is blending
  several branches, which can occur for mixed or previously unseen chemistries.
\end{itemize}

BranchWeightMLP is a composition-conditioned branch router: it depends only on the chemical
composition of a system, so its routing weights are invariant to atomic
displacements and therefore constant along an \ac{MD} trajectory. The signal
thus distinguishes \emph{which systems (chemistries)} are routed diffusely, not
\emph{which configurations} within a fixed-composition trajectory are critical.
In \textsc{matsim-agents}, its entropy and top-weight statistics are therefore
used as a low-cost, system-level triage heuristic for agentic control: sharply
routed chemistries can proceed to analysis, whereas diffusely routed
chemistries can trigger additional validation or \acs{DFT} labeling
(Fig.~\ref{fig:branchuq}).

\subsubsection{Active Learning Loop}
When the \acs{UQ} gate triggers, \textsc{matsim-agents} executes a configurable
number of active learning iterations. Each iteration proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Candidate generation}: short \ac{MD} trajectories (\acs{ASE} Langevin or
  \ac{NVT}) are run from seed structures to sample configuration space; frames are
  collected as candidates.
\item \textbf{System-level uncertainty scoring}: because the BranchWeightMLP
  routing signal depends only on chemical composition, it is constant along a
  given trajectory and is used to flag which candidate \emph{systems}
  (compositions) are routed diffusely and therefore warrant escalation, rather
  than to rank individual frames within a trajectory. For backends without
  branch routing (e.g.\ UMA), or when configuration-level scoring is preferred,
  candidates can instead be scored by \ac{MC}-Dropout force variance or by
  deep-ensemble disagreement, including a combined ensemble-then-dropout
  strategy.
\item \textbf{Within-trajectory snapshot selection}: for each flagged system,
  critical snapshots are drawn from its \ac{MD} frames using a greedy
  farthest-point diversity filter over configuration descriptors, which removes
  near-duplicate frames and retains geometrically distinct configurations.
\item \textbf{\acs{DFT} labeling}: the top-$n$ selected candidates are submitted to
  the user-selected \acs{DFT} backend, either \acs{QE} or \acs{VASP}, via
  batch job submission. \acs{QE} is open source, whereas \acs{VASP} is a licensed
  closed-source code that requires user-provided source access. Converged frames
  are converted to the extended-XYZ format.
\item \textbf{\acs{MLIP} retraining}: the new frames are appended to the training
  dataset and the selected \acs{MLIP} backend is retrained for a configurable
  number of epochs---HydraGNN is retrained from its checkpoint, while UMA is
  optionally fine-tuned from its foundation-model checkpoint---through a
  user-supplied training script or multi-node launcher. When retraining is
  disabled, UMA is used as a frozen foundation model and the labeled frames are
  simply accumulated for offline fine-tuning.
\item \textbf{Loop state serialization}: a \texttt{state.json} per iteration
  records candidate/selection counts, \acs{UQ} statistics, \acs{DFT} convergence rates,
  and timing. Fault-tolerant resume logic skips completed iterations.
\end{enumerate}

The loop is backend-agnostic: the \acs{DFT} backend interface accepts a
\texttt{DFTJobSpec} and returns a list of converged frames, allowing \acs{QE} and
\acs{VASP} to be used interchangeably from the workflow perspective. This design gives
users the option to rely on fully open-source \acs{QE} when software licensing and
reproducibility are priorities, or to use \acs{VASP} when a project requires an
established licensed production code and the user has access to the \acs{VASP} source
and pseudopotential files.

\subsubsection{DFT Warm-Start Protocol}
A separate warm-start benchmarking module is evaluated as a factorial
backend ablation that pairs each \acs{MLIP} backend with each \acs{DFT} solver:
\texttt{warmstart\_benchmark\_qe} targets \acs{QE} while
\texttt{warmstart\_benchmark\_vasp} targets \acs{VASP}, and each module accepts
either HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} (graph-neural-network \acs{GFM}) or
UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} (universal foundation-model potential) as the
pre-relaxation backend. Enumerating both choices yields four benchmark
configurations---HydraGNN+\acs{QE}, HydraGNN+\acs{VASP}, UMA+\acs{QE}, and
UMA+\acs{VASP}---enabling direct comparison of warm-start benefit across both
\acs{MLIP} families and both \acs{DFT} solvers.

Beyond its primary role as a convergence accelerator, the cold-start
vs.\ warm-start comparison fulfills two broader functions within the agentic
workflow. First, it provides a computational validation safety net: if the
\acs{MLIP}-relaxed structure genuinely brings atoms closer to the \acs{DFT} energy
minimum, the warm run should require fewer \acs{BFGS} steps and fewer \acs{SCF}
iterations than the cold run; failure of this condition signals that the \acs{MLIP}
prediction may not be trustworthy for that chemistry. Second, systematic
collection of such failure cases across the benchmark fixture suite identifies
\emph{regions of \acs{MLIP} weakness} in materials space---compositions or
structural motifs where the model's pre-relaxation is unreliable---and this
information can be used to target active-learning data collection and improve
model robustness.

The benchmark pipeline, shared across all four configurations, proceeds as follows
for any input crystal structure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Run \acs{MLIP} \acs{FIRE} pre-relaxation to $f_\text{max} = 0.01$\,eV/\AA\ using
  either HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} or UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} (\acs{MLIP} warm start).
\item Run \acs{QE} \texttt{vc-relax} from the \emph{original} coordinates (cold
  start).
\item Run \acs{QE} \texttt{vc-relax} from the \emph{\acs{MLIP}-relaxed} coordinates
  (warm start).
\item Record and compare \acs{BFGS} step counts, per-step \acs{SCF} iteration counts,
  total \acs{SCF} iterations, final energies, and wall times.
\end{enumerate}

The same \acs{QE} input settings are used for both cold and warm runs to ensure a
fair comparison. The benchmark writes a structured \ac{JSON} summary alongside the
pw.x output files, enabling automated regression testing and reproducible
reporting.

\section{HPC Deployment}
\label{sec:hpc}

\subsection{Platform-Agnostic Design Principles}
The \textsc{matsim-agents} software stack is designed around three portability
principles:

\textbf{Module isolation.} All \acs{GPU}-software interactions (\acs{CUDA} version, \acs{MPI}
implementation, compiler toolchain) are encapsulated behind per-platform shell
fragments. A single function \texttt{load\_perlmutter\_modules\_gpu()} (or its
Frontier/Aurora equivalents) sets up the entire environment from a clean login
shell, making jobs bit-for-bit reproducible across re-submissions.

\textbf{Decoupled Python and \ac{MPI} environments.} The \acs{MLIP} backends---HydraGNN,
UMA, and MACE-MP---require a PyTorch \acs{GPU} environment, while first-principles calculations require separate
\acs{MPI}/compiler stacks for \acs{QE} or \acs{VASP}. These are kept in separate shells: the batch
script activates the Python environment for the selected \acs{MLIP} phase and spawns the
\acs{DFT} launcher as a subprocess that loads the appropriate compiler, \acs{MPI}, \acs{GPU}
runtime, and code-specific environment for the selected backend.

\textbf{vLLM inference serving.} \acs{LLM} inference is handled by a vLLM server
running in a separate Slurm allocation (or as a background process on a login
node for interactive sessions). The framework communicates with the server via
an OpenAI-compatible \ac{HTTP} endpoint, requiring only the \texttt{openai} Python
package on the client side.

\subsection{User-Selectable DFT Backends and Build Scripts}
\textsc{matsim-agents} exposes the first-principles code as a user-selectable
backend rather than hard-coding a single \acs{DFT} engine. The two supported options
are \acs{QE} and \acs{VASP}. \acs{QE} is open source and can be built,
modified, and redistributed according to its license. \acs{VASP} is a licensed
closed-source code; therefore, the framework can provide build and launch
scripts, but users must supply their own authorized \acs{VASP} source tree,
pseudopotential files, and institutional license access.

To make both options usable on leadership-class facilities, the repository
includes detailed bash scripts for compiling \acs{QE} and \acs{VASP} on \acs{NERSC} Perlmutter,
\acs{ALCF} Aurora, and \acs{OLCF} Frontier. These scripts document the module loads,
compiler choices, \acs{MPI} settings, \acs{GPU} offload configuration, and launcher
conventions required on each system. They are intended to reduce the amount of
site-specific trial-and-error normally required to deploy first-principles codes
inside an automated \acs{AI} workflow.

\subsection{Per-Platform Deployment}
The framework runs on all three \acs{DOE} leadership-class systems, each with a
distinct accelerator and \acs{QE} offload backend. On Perlmutter (NVIDIA A100),
\acs{QE} is built with NVHPC using \acs{CUDA} \ac{FFTW} offload and \ac{ELPA}
diagonalisation, and \acs{GPU}-aware \acs{MPI} is disabled for the single-node
warm-start benchmark to avoid \ac{GTL} startup overhead. On Frontier (AMD
MI250X), \acs{QE} uses OpenMP target offload through the HIP backend, with the
module fragment masking \texttt{ROCR\_VISIBLE\_DEVICES} to expose individual
\acp{GCD}. On Aurora (Intel \acs{PVC}), the \texttt{frameworks} module supplies
vLLM and \ac{XPU} PyTorch without a source build, \acs{QE} uses the OneAPI/OpenMP
offload backend, and a custom entrypoint wrapper works around a vLLM
model-registry SIGSEGV. HydraGNN runs on the platform-native PyTorch build for
each accelerator (\acs{CUDA}, ROCm/HIP, and \acs{XPU}/OneAPI, respectively); the
UMA and MACE-MP foundation-model backends are currently deployed on the
Perlmutter \acs{CUDA} build. Companion scripts build \emph{both} first-principles engines---\acs{QE}
and \acs{VASP}---on all three facilities (\acs{VASP} requiring the user's
licensed source), and the active-learning loop is wired end-to-end on Perlmutter
and Frontier, with the Aurora compile/runtime stack in place.

\subsection{Warm-Start Benchmark Setup}
\label{sec:results:warmstart_setup}
The warm-start benchmarks are organized as a controlled comparison between two
ways of initializing each first-principles geometry optimization:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cold start.} The \acs{DFT} optimizer is launched directly from the
  \emph{original} input coordinates of the fixture, with no \acs{MLIP}
  pre-processing. This is the conventional baseline.
\item \textbf{Warm start.} The same fixture is first relaxed by an \acs{MLIP}
  to $f_\text{max} = 0.01$\,eV/\AA, and the \acs{DFT} optimizer is then launched
  from the \emph{\acs{MLIP}-relaxed} coordinates. The \acs{DFT} settings are held
  identical to the cold run so that any difference in convergence is
  attributable solely to the change of initial geometry.
\end{itemize}

The cold/warm pair is run as a \emph{factorial backend ablation}: the same
cold/warm comparison is repeated for every combination of two independent
backend choices. The first choice is the \acs{MLIP} pre-relaxation engine
(HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} or UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}); the second is the \acs{DFT}
solver (\acs{QE} or \acs{VASP}). Enumerating both choices yields four backend
pairings---HydraGNN+\acs{QE}, HydraGNN+\acs{VASP}, UMA+\acs{QE}, and
UMA+\acs{VASP}---each run in both cold and warm modes. Because only one backend
factor is varied at a time while the other is held fixed, a warm-start benefit
(or its absence) can be attributed to the \acs{MLIP}, to the \acs{DFT} code, or to
their interaction---the defining property of an ablation study.

Three alloy fixtures provide the off-equilibrium ``hard cases'' that make the
cold/warm distinction meaningful: MoNbTaW \acs{HEA} (16-atom BCC supercell),
NbTaW BCC, and NbMoW BCC. A Si diamond fixture is included as a high-symmetry
reference case that establishes baseline correctness of the pipeline before the
alloy benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:results:warmstart_si}). For every
fixture$\times$backend combination, both the cold and warm runs are repeated
three times on Perlmutter to assess run-to-run reproducibility, and the
following quantities are recorded and compared between the cold and warm runs:
the number of \acs{BFGS} ionic steps, the per-step and total \acs{SCF} iteration
counts, the final total energy, and the wall time.

As described in Section~\ref{sec:architecture}, this cold-start vs.\ warm-start
comparison serves two roles beyond raw convergence acceleration: it is a
computational validation safety net for the \acs{MLIP} (a genuine geometry
improvement must reduce \acs{BFGS} steps and \acs{SCF} iterations), and it is a
probe for regions of \acs{MLIP} weakness (fixtures where warm-start benefit is
absent or where \acs{DFT} convergence degrades) whose failure cases can be routed
back into the active learning loop.

The random-alloy site occupation in these structures creates genuine
site-specific forces at the input geometry, so the \acs{MLIP} pre-relaxation must
move atoms before converging and the warm-start coordinates differ meaningfully
from the cold-start input. Each fixture is evaluated under all four backend
combinations (HydraGNN+\acs{QE}, HydraGNN+\acs{VASP}, UMA+\acs{QE}, UMA+\acs{VASP}),

the \acs{MLIP} pre-relaxes atoms toward their local chemical environment

benefit: comparing the two \acs{MLIP} rows for a fixed \acs{DFT} solver isolates

\emph{increases} \acs{QE} cost ($\Delta_\text{SCF}=+68\%$ on NbTaW, where the

\emph{not} to advocate for any single \acs{MLIP}: it is to host multiple
potentials behind a uniform, \acs{HPC}-portable interface so that the workflow
can \emph{measure}, per material, how each model behaves and act on that
measurement. On these alloy fixtures UMA pre-relaxation reduces
$\Delta_\text{SCF}$ on both \acs{DFT} columns (up to the $37\%$ reduction on
UMA+\acs{VASP}/NbMoW), whereas HydraGNN pre-relaxation is neutral on \acs{VASP}
($0\%$ on all three fixtures) and, on NbTaW+\acs{QE}, increases cost ($+68\%$).

measurement, and the ranking can invert on a different material. The same
cold/warm instrument that accelerates \acs{DFT} when a potential is well matched
doubles as a model-selection and weakness-detection probe when it is not: the
$+68\%$ result is not a defect of the framework but exactly the signal it is
built to surface, flagging that fixture as out-of-distribution for that potential
and routing it back into the active learning loop as a target for new data.
Because the backend is a user-selectable configuration rather than a hard-coded
dependency, the same run can switch potentials, benchmark several against one
another on identical fixtures, combine them as a mixture of experts, or use any
of them as the pretrained starting point for active-learning data
collection---none of which requires a change to the agentic orchestration, the
\acs{DFT} escalation logic, or the deployment scripts.

\subsection{End-to-End Integration and Throughput}
\label{sec:results:al}
The central systems claim of \textsc{matsim-agents} is that its components---
seed generation, \acs{MLIP}-driven \acs{MD} sampling, uncertainty-based
acquisition, \acs{DFT} labelling, dataset assembly, and model
retraining---compose into a single automated loop that runs unattended on
leadership \acs{HPC}. To demonstrate this we executed the active-learning loop
end to end on five of the six paper cases (Cu-BHT again pending a \acs{CIF}
seed), on \acs{NERSC} Perlmutter using the OpenACC \acs{GPU} build of
\acs{VASP}~6.6.0 on NVIDIA A100 nodes. Each iteration proceeds without human
intervention: seeds are propagated by short Langevin \acs{MD} (400 steps,
2\,fs, 1000\,K) under the \acs{MLIP} force engine; snapshots are sub-sampled
every 15 steps and screened by force and displacement thresholds; the surviving
candidates are scored by \acs{MC}-dropout and down-selected under a per-iteration
budget with a composition-diversity filter; the selected frames are labelled by
concurrent single-point \acs{VASP} jobs; and the converged frames are appended
to the growing training set. State is checkpointed per iteration so runs resume
after walltime limits.

Table~\ref{tab:al} reports per-case throughput. Across all cases the loop drove
the full pipeline hands-free, dispatching \acs{DFT} jobs concurrently within a

Figure~\ref{fig:al_timing} decomposes the per-iteration wall time by stage.
Single-point \acs{DFT} labelling accounts for 88--97\% of the wall clock in
every case, while the entire agentic orchestration layer---building the
\acs{MLIP} calculator, running the Langevin \acs{MD} sampler, scoring
candidates by \acs{MC}-dropout, appending the labelled frames, and invoking the
retrain hook---contributes only 3--12\%. The acquisition and dataset-management
stages are sub-second to a few seconds per iteration (e.g.\ 2.6\,s acquisition
and 0.15\,s append for the Cantor \acs{HEA}); the only non-trivial non-DFT cost
is \acs{MLIP}-\acs{MD} sampling, which scales with system size and step count.
Wrapping the first-principles engine in the automated loop is therefore close to
free: the framework spends its time where the physics is, not in coordination.

The loop is also resilient to the wall-clock limits that dominate shared
\acs{HPC} scheduling. State is checkpointed after every iteration (a
per-iteration \texttt{state.json} plus the growing \texttt{dataset.extxyz}), so
a job that is pre-empted or hits its wall-time limit resumes from the last
completed iteration on resubmission with no manual bookkeeping. The
LiFePO\textsubscript{4} run in Table~\ref{tab:al}, for instance, was interrupted
by a wall-time limit after five iterations and completed the remaining five in a
second allocation, reusing every previously labelled frame. This
checkpoint-and-resume behaviour lets long campaigns span many short,
backfill-friendly allocations rather than requiring a single monolithic
reservation.

Two scope notes accompany these numbers. First, the acquisition stage ran in
effective pass-through mode for most cases: with the frozen \acs{MLIP}
checkpoint used here the injected \acs{MC}-dropout produced near-zero force
variance, and for four of the five cases the number of screened \acs{MD}
candidates fell at or below the per-iteration budget, so essentially every valid
frame was labelled. The BCC \acs{HEA} is the exception: it generated more
candidates (1650) than its budget (1000 selected), so the diversity filter
actively down-selected. Second, the two $\dagger$ rows are partial snapshots of
runs still executing at the time of writing. Consequently these results should
be read as a demonstration of \emph{integration and throughput}---that every
stage is wired together and runs autonomously at \acs{HPC} scale---rather than
as an uncertainty-driven learning curve; the uncertainty-guided acquisition and
in-loop retraining components are present and exercised, and quantifying their
sample efficiency with a trainable ensemble backend is the natural next study.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning on Active-Learning-Corrected Data}
\label{sec:results:finetune}
The end-to-end loop of Section~\ref{sec:results:al} produces, for each paper
case, a compact set of \acs{DFT}-labelled frames drawn precisely from the
configurations the \acs{MLIP} itself sampled during \acs{MD}. The natural
question that closes the loop is whether re-training the force engine on this
active-learning-corrected data measurably improves it on the very chemistry that
generated the frames. We therefore ran a controlled \emph{fine-tune-and-evaluate}
study on five of the six paper cases (Cu-BHT again pending a \acs{CIF} seed),
using the per-case datasets accumulated by the loop
(LiFePO\textsubscript{4}, Cantor FCC \acs{HEA}, NbTaVHfZrTi BCC \acs{HEA},
phosphorene, and the Zn(HCOO)\textsubscript{2} \acs{MOF}).

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Each dataset is split into disjoint train and held-out test partitions. We then
measure a strict \emph{before/after} pair on the identical test partition: the
\emph{before} endpoint is the pretrained checkpoint evaluated with no exposure to
the case data, and the \emph{after} endpoint is the same architecture
fine-tuned on the train partition. Because both endpoints are scored on the same
held-out frames with the same metrics, any change is attributable solely to the
fine-tuning. We report two error metrics per endpoint: the force mean absolute
error (\acs{MAE}, in eV/\AA) and the per-atom energy \acs{MAE} after removing a
constant reference shift (in meV/atom), the latter isolating the shape of the
energy surface from a trivial global offset.

\paragraph{Backends.}
The study spans all three \acs{MLIP} engines used elsewhere in the paper. For
HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} we contrast four transfer-learning strategies against a
common baseline: (i) \emph{routed}, the default multi-head \ac{GFM} routing;
(ii) \emph{unfrozen}, a full fine-tune of the backbone and heads; (iii)
\emph{frozen}, in which the backbone is held fixed and only the output head is
retrained; and (iv) \emph{scratch}, a model trained from random initialization
on the case data alone, which isolates the value of the pretrained
representation. For UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} we fine-tune directly through the
model's conservative energy-and-force head with a lightweight custom training
loop---a small number of Adam epochs on a force-weighted energy-and-force loss
that updates the pretrained output heads in place---in three variants:
\emph{full} (all trainable weights), \emph{frozen} (backbone held fixed, output
head only), and \emph{LoRA}, in which low-rank adapters are inserted into the
backbone's scalar and radial linear layers, leaving the equivariant tensor
operations untouched. For MACE~\cite{batatia2022,batatia2023macemp} we fine-tune
the released MACE-MP foundation models at all three published capacities
(\emph{small}, \emph{medium}, \emph{large}) using the authors' own training
entry point, in two variants: a \emph{naive} full fine-tune and a native
\emph{LoRA} fine-tune. All fine-tunes adapt the large pretrained potential to a
narrow domain without erasing its general knowledge, and every backend is scored
on the same held-out partition with identical metrics so the comparison is
strictly like-for-like.

\paragraph{Execution.}
The full matrix---three foundation-model backends at their several fine-tuning
variants, together with the HydraGNN transfer-learning strategies, each across
five cases---amounts to dozens of independent single-\acs{GPU}
fine-tune-and-evaluate campaigns. To use leadership-class nodes efficiently we
execute them through a packed launcher that co-schedules four self-contained
campaigns on the four \acs{GPU} devices of a single node, pinning one campaign
per device and collecting per-campaign logs and evaluation outputs
independently. Because every campaign uses identical dataset splits, metrics,
and held-out partitions regardless of how it is scheduled, packing four per node
quadruples node-level throughput without altering any individual result, and the
entire table is reproduced by resubmitting the same packed jobs.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning through the conservative force head.}
A subtle but decisive methodological choice surfaced when fine-tuning the
UMA foundation model. UMA predicts forces as the analytic gradient of its energy
with respect to atomic positions---a \emph{conservative} force head evaluated
inside the forward pass. The convenient route of driving the fine-tune through
the reference configuration pipeline instead recomputes the output
normalizers---the force \acs{RMS} scale and the per-element linear energy
references---from the (small) case dataset and trains a \emph{direct}-force head.
On a few hundred frames these statistics are estimated far from their
pretraining values: the force scale inflates by roughly a factor of three and
the per-element references collapse onto the handful of elements present in the
case. Because a direct-force head multiplies its raw output by the stored force
scale, a gentle fine-tune that barely moves the network weights nonetheless
emits forces mis-scaled by that same factor, so accuracy \emph{degrades} from
the first optimization step. We therefore fine-tune the conservative head
\emph{directly}: the model is run in training mode so the head retains the
double-backprop graph needed to differentiate the force gradient, and a
force-weighted energy-and-force loss updates the pretrained heads while leaving
their output statistics untouched. We adopt this convention for all
foundation-model fine-tunes reported here, and note it explicitly because the
normalizer-refitting route is an easy and silent failure mode when adapting
large pretrained potentials to small domain datasets.

\paragraph{Cross-backend results.}
Table~\ref{tab:finetune} reports the held-out force and reference-shifted energy
\acs{MAE} for every backend and fine-tuning variant on the five
active-learning-corrected paper cases, each grouped under its zero-shot
baseline. Because all methods are scored on the same held-out frames with the
same metrics, the table is a strictly like-for-like comparison of HydraGNN, UMA,
and the three MACE-MP capacities. The pretrained potentials all start
off-distribution on these specialized chemistries (battery cathode, refractory
and structural \acsp{HEA}, 2D semiconductor, porous \acs{MOF}), none of which is
well represented in their training distributions. Fine-tuning on the
loop-generated data reduces both error metrics in essentially every case.

For HydraGNN the full fine-tune (\emph{unfrozen}) and the
from-\emph{scratch} specialist give the largest error reductions and are
typically close to each other; the \emph{frozen}-backbone (head-only) update
yields a smaller reduction, and the default multi-head \emph{routed} update
changes the metrics least. That the from-scratch specialist is competitive with
transfer learning indicates the active-learning-corrected datasets are internally
consistent enough to train a capable case-specific potential on their own. For
UMA the same instrument contrasts full fine-tuning against
its parameter-efficient alternatives (frozen backbone with head-only training,
and LoRA); for the MACE-MP capacities---whose native training entry point does
not expose head-only backbone freezing---it contrasts naive full fine-tuning
against LoRA, and, across the three capacities, whether the additional
foundation-model size translates into lower post-fine-tune error on these narrow
domains. The MACE fine-tunes are produced through the authors'
native training entry point (identical energy/force loss, exponential
moving-average weight tracking, and gradient clipping as the reference recipe)
so the numbers are directly
comparable to community MACE-MP fine-tuning practice.

We report these numbers as measurements, not as a ranking that anoints a single
best potential. Which backend and which adaptation give the lowest error depend
on the material and the metric, and the absolute values shift as more
active-learning data is collected, so a result on one fixture does not transfer
to a general recommendation. \textsc{matsim-agents} therefore treats
Table~\ref{tab:finetune} as a benchmarking instrument rather than an
endorsement: the same uniform interface lets a user switch potentials, benchmark
several against one another on identical held-out frames, combine them as a
mixture of experts, or take any of them as the pretrained starting point for
further active-learning data collection. The comparison is thus kept factual and
the choice of potential remains the user's, per material and per task.

\subsection{Hypothesis Formulation and Multi-LLM Debate}
\label{sec:results:hypothesis}
The fine-tuning results above quantify \emph{how accurate} each surrogate is on
the corrected data; this subsection closes the loop by showing how that accuracy
propagates into the \emph{scientific conclusion} that the interface layer draws.
We exercise the optional multi-\acs{LLM} peer review of the discovery-chat entry
point (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) on a materials-characterization task:
given an element set and a family of stoichiometries, decide which
crystallographic structure is simultaneously \emph{chemically} stable
(formation energy on or below the convex hull) and \emph{dynamically} stable
(no imaginary phonon modes), and state the numerical criterion that would
falsify the claim. A proposer model (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) drafts a falsifiable
hypothesis, and a panel of two critics from independent developers
(DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B and Gemma~4~31B; Table~\ref{tab:llms}) revises it
over two rounds of critique and cross-critique. All three models are served
locally through vLLM on a single node (three A100-80GB \acsp{GPU}, one model
each); the driver and \acs{HPC} launcher are released with the code. The only
evidence the panel is given about the surrogate is its measured held-out
accuracy from Table~\ref{tab:finetune}; the panel is not told which potential to
prefer, so any change in its verdict is a function of the reported numbers alone.

We run three fixtures---the two \acs{HEA}s and the LiFePO$_4$ olivine/maricite
cathode---under two evidence conditions: the zero-shot
accuracy (\emph{before} active-learning fine-tuning) and the fine-tuned accuracy
(\emph{after})---and compare the resulting hypotheses (Table~\ref{tab:hypothesis}).
The panel consistently anchors its decision to the physical fact that competing
polymorphs are separated by only tens of meV/atom, so a surrogate whose energy
error exceeds that spacing cannot resolve the convex hull. For the Cantor
\acs{HEA}, the before-fine-tuning evidence gives every backend an energy
\acs{MAE} of 150--2248~meV/atom; the panel judges all three surrogates
insufficient to rank the FCC/HCP/BCC alternatives and recommends escalating to
\acs{DFT} and routing the structures back into active learning. Under the
after-fine-tuning evidence the most accurate fine-tuned potential on that
fixture (MACE-MP-L, 3~meV/atom) falls below the polymorph spacing; the panel now
asserts the single-phase FCC solid solution, tightens the falsification
tolerance to $\pm 10$~meV/atom and a residual-force bound of $0.07$~eV/\AA, and
adds an ensemble-uncertainty gate that still escalates individual structures
whose cross-model energy spread exceeds $5$~meV/atom. For the refractory
NbTaVHfZrTi \acs{HEA} the shift is more nuanced: because UMA is already accurate
before fine-tuning ($5$~meV/atom), the panel separates the two notions of
stability, treating chemical stability as resolvable from the surrogate energies
while insisting that dynamical (phonon) stability be confirmed in \acs{DFT}
until the force error is small enough to trust vibrational signs---which the
fine-tuned potentials ($\le\!0.005$~eV/\AA) subsequently provide. The
LiFePO$_4$ cathode (olivine \emph{Pnma} versus maricite) exposes a third pattern
together with a benchmarking caveat. Before fine-tuning no backend is usable (best
energy \acs{MAE} 97~meV/atom), and the panel additionally recognises the known
phase-separating character of Li$_x$FePO$_4$, posing a miscibility-gap
counter-hypothesis before escalating to \acs{DFT} and active learning. After
fine-tuning only MACE-MP-L improves (energy 9~meV/atom, force $0.021$~eV/\AA),
whereas UMA and HydraGNN in fact \emph{regress} on this chemistry---a divergence
the before/after benchmark surfaces automatically; even then the panel asserts
only chemical resolvability while still escalating the phonon (dynamical-stability)
check to \acs{DFT}, because $0.021$~eV/\AA\ remains above the
$\sim\!0.005$~eV/\AA\ it requires to trust vibrational signs.

Two points matter for the framework rather than for the specific chemistries.
First, the conclusion the system reports is not fixed: the same prompt and the
same panel move from ``escalate to \acs{DFT}/active learning'' to ``assert the
stable structure, with an uncertainty gate'' purely because the underlying
\acs{MLIP} became more accurate through the active-learning loop, which is the
intended coupling between the physics layer and the reasoning layer. The same
coupling also acts defensively: on LiFePO$_4$ the panel withholds the
dynamical-stability claim while the fine-tuned force error is still too coarse for
phonons, and the before/after evidence itself exposes cases where fine-tuning
\emph{degrades} a universal potential rather than improving it. Second, the
multi-model panel reproduces, in natural language, the same uncertainty-aware
escalation logic that the \acs{UQ} gate applies numerically
(Section~\ref{sec:results:uq}): it declines to over-claim when the evidence is
weak and narrows its criteria only when the measured error supports it. The
panel is a cross-examination step that surfaces this reasoning explicitly, not
an oracle that replaces the physical calculations.

\subsection{Strong Scaling of DFT Labeling}
\label{sec:results:scaling}
The per-iteration breakdown of Fig.~\ref{fig:al_timing} shows that
single-point \acs{DFT} labelling accounts for 88--97\% of the loop's wall time,
which raises a natural systems question: how does that dominant stage respond
to additional node allocation? To answer it we ran a controlled strong-scaling
study in which a \emph{fixed} 32-frame workload of FCC \acs{HEA}
configurations---drawn from CrMnFeCoNi and four near-equimolar FCC
compositions---is labelled by single-point \acs{VASP} at four node counts,
$N\in\{1,2,4,8\}$. Concurrency is expressed as $N$ simultaneous one-node
\acs{VASP} jobs (four A100 ranks each), so $N$ nodes process the same 32 frames
with $N$-way parallelism. Because the workload is held fixed while the resource
grows, this is a strong-scaling (fixed-problem) measurement.

To make the result defensible against per-draw variability and the stochastic
\acs{SCF} cost of individual random-alloy frames, we repeat the entire sweep
$R=5$ times. Each repeat draws an independent random 32-frame workload (seeded
deterministically and held fixed across node counts within the repeat), and the
scaling metric is measured \emph{inside} each job from its per-iteration
\texttt{state.json}: the work-normalized \acs{DFT} throughput is the number of
converged frames divided by the cumulative \acs{DFT} wall time,
$\text{throughput} = n_\text{conv}/t_\text{DFT}\times 3600$ frames/h. Because
throughput is computed from in-job timers rather than queue-to-queue latency, it
is independent of the Slurm QOS used to schedule the jobs, so premium- and
regular-QOS runs contribute to the same dataset without bias. Parallel
efficiency is $100\times(\text{throughput}_N/N)/(\text{throughput}_1/1)$ relative
to the single-node baseline.

Figure~\ref{fig:al_dft_scaling} reports the mean throughput and parallel
efficiency over the five repeats. Throughput rises from $18.1\pm4.6$ frames/h on
one node to $28.0\pm7.3$ (two nodes), $53.6\pm13.3$ (four nodes), and
$113.5\pm40.5$ (eight nodes), corresponding to speedups of $1.55\times$,
$2.96\times$, and $6.28\times$. Parallel efficiency remains between 74\% and
79\% across the scaled runs.

The remaining sublinear scaling is a property of the \emph{fixed} workload
rather than a coordination bottleneck in the orchestration layer. The random
alloy configurations differ widely in \acs{SCF} difficulty---some converge in a
handful of iterations, others exhaust their \acs{SCF} budget or fail---so at high
concurrency the stage makespan is still influenced by whichever frame is slowest
within a repeat. Increasing the fixed workload from the pilot 16-frame problem to
32 frames reduces this straggler sensitivity: at eight nodes the mean throughput
rises from 66.9 to 113.5 frames/h and the relative standard deviation drops from
62\% to 36\%, yielding substantially steadier high-concurrency behavior. Because
\acs{DFT} labelling is embarrassingly parallel---each frame is an independent
single-point job---the 74--79\% efficiencies from two through eight nodes show
that \textsc{matsim-agents} introduces little scheduling overhead; the residual
gap to ideal scaling reflects finite batch granularity and stochastic \acs{SCF}
cost rather than contention in the orchestration layer. In production this
argues for matching the node allocation to the labelling batch: larger
acquisition budgets can exploit higher concurrency effectively, whereas very
small batches become straggler-bound once the number of workers approaches the
number of difficult frames.

\subsection{Backend and Platform Portability of the Loop}
\label{sec:results:portability}
The loop treats both the \acs{MLIP} and the first-principles engine as
configuration-selected backends rather than hard-coded dependencies, so the
same orchestration runs across accelerators and \acs{DFT} codes without code
changes. Table~\ref{tab:portability} summarises the coverage. On Perlmutter the
identical phosphorene loop labels structures with either \acs{VASP} or \acs{QE}:
only the \texttt{dft:} block of the configuration differs
(\texttt{al\_phosphorene.yaml} vs.\ \texttt{al\_phosphorene\_qe.yaml}), and each
first-principles code is reached through a thin per-platform step launcher
(\texttt{\_vasp-step-perlmutter.sh}, \texttt{\_qe-step-perlmutter.sh}) that
performs its own module swap, so the driver's Python/\acs{MLIP} environment
never collides with the compiler/\acs{MPI} stack of the \acs{DFT} code. The same
abstraction carries the loop to \acs{OLCF} Frontier (AMD MI250X): the Frontier
\acs{QE} step launcher and active-learning orchestrator are in place, and the
corresponding configuration (\texttt{al\_phosphorene\_qe\_frontier.yaml}) differs
from the Perlmutter one only in paths and the eight-\acs{GCD} rank/thread layout.

of Section~\ref{sec:hpc}, which also covers the Aurora (Intel \acs{PVC}) stack to

\section{Conclusions and Future Developments}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\subsection{Symmetry and the Warm-Start Benefit}
The Si diamond benchmark illuminates an important subtlety for warm-start
workflows: \emph{atomic forces alone do not capture all structural degrees of
freedom}. For high-symmetry crystals, ionic forces are exactly zero by symmetry
at \emph{any} lattice parameter, and the entire relaxation problem reduces to
optimising the unit-cell volume (or shape). \acs{FIRE}, which is an ionic optimizer,
will always converge in one step for such structures regardless of how far
the cell is from equilibrium. The warm-start benefit therefore vanishes for
high-symmetry elemental and binary systems.

The benefit reappears for: (1) low-symmetry structures where internal
coordinates are genuinely off-equilibrium (HEAs, substitutionally disordered
alloys, defect supercells, MOFs); (2) structures undergoing symmetry-breaking
phase transitions; and (3) molecular crystals with flexible organic linkers.
Fixture selection for warm-start benchmarks should prioritise these categories.

\subsection{Scalability and Cost}
On Perlmutter, the \acs{UQ} benchmark for three structures costs $\approx$14\,min
of \acs{GPU} time (one A100 node), dominated by HydraGNN model loading ($\sim$60\,s)
rather than inference ($<$1\,s per structure). Amortised over a production
discovery run of hundreds of structures, model loading cost is negligible.
The \acs{QE} warm-start benchmark for Si costs $\approx$6\,min including both \acs{DFT}
runs; the MoNbTaW benchmark (16 atoms, metallic, vc-relax) is expected to
require $\approx$20--40\,min per \acs{DFT} run at standard convergence thresholds.

\subsection{LLM Provider Flexibility}
A deliberate design decision is provider-agnosticism: the framework supports
Ollama, vLLM, OpenAI, Anthropic, and HuggingFace transformers backends.
On leadership-class machines without internet access, vLLM with locally
downloaded open-weight models is the standard deployment. The models directory
currently provides ten checkpoints spanning several independent developers, a
wide parameter range, and both dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures
(Table~\ref{tab:llms}). This selection lets the planning and analysis agents be
matched to the available accelerator memory and the desired quality/cost
tradeoff, rather than being tied to a single vendor or model.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Developments}
We have presented \textsc{matsim-agents}, an open-source agentic \acs{AI} scientific
workflow framework whose central novelty is the closed-loop orchestration of
natural-language scientific intent, \acs{MLIP}-based atomistic execution,
uncertainty-aware validation, selective \acs{DFT} escalation, active learning, and
language-level scientific synthesis on leadership-class \acs{HPC} systems. The system integrates \ac{LLM} planning, \acs{MLIP} relaxation (HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn},
UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}, and MACE-MP~\cite{batatia2023macemp}), \acs{UQ}-gated decision making, active learning,
first-principles escalation, and \acs{DFT} warm-starting into a unified, portable
pipeline designed for \acs{NERSC} Perlmutter, \acs{OLCF} Frontier, and \acs{ALCF} Aurora. It
addresses all five subtopics of the ``Scientific Workflows \& Application
Development'' theme of the AgenticAI4HPC workshop: intelligent workflow
composition through a checkpointed LangGraph multi-agent graph; iterative
optimisation through a self-improving active learning loop; agent-assisted
validation through the \acs{UQ} gate; \acs{AI}-driven algorithm design through the
branch-weight uncertainty mechanism; and tight integration of simulation (\acs{DFT}),
data analytics (\acs{MLIP} inference), and \acs{AI} (\acs{LLM} planning) workloads.
Benchmark results on Perlmutter confirm correct \acs{UQ} behaviour for representative
crystal classes and validate the warm-start benchmarking infrastructure for
genuinely off-equilibrium structures such as the MoNbTaW \acs{HEA}, positioning
\textsc{matsim-agents} as a practical framework for agentic \acs{AI} workflows in
demanding computational materials science on leadership-class \acs{HPC}.

These demonstrated capabilities also delineate the current boundaries of the
framework, and each boundary is an opportunity for the next stage of development.
The present limitations are: (1) automatic in-loop retraining is demonstrated
end-to-end for HydraGNN, whereas the foundation-model backends
(UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} and MACE-MP~\cite{batatia2023macemp}) are supported as
relaxation engines and through the offline fine-tune study of
Section~\ref{sec:results:finetune}, and are wired as in-loop retrain hooks, but
have not yet been exercised as fully automatic in-loop retraining steps; the
warm-start module is backend-agnostic and supports HydraGNN, UMA, and MACE-MP as
pre-relaxation backends; (2) the warm-start protocol handles ionic coordinates but not cell
shape/volume, which limits benefit for pure-strain relaxation problems; (3) the
\acs{LLM} planner does not yet incorporate literature search or database queries
(Materials Project, AFLOW) as tool calls; and (4) the present \acs{UQ} gate relies
on the BranchWeightMLP routing heuristic, which is composition-conditioned and
therefore constant across atomistic configurations of the same chemical system.

These limitations map directly onto our planned developments. To address the
configuration-insensitivity of the gate, we will promote the already-implemented
\ac{MC}-Dropout and deep-ensemble scorers---presently used for candidate
acquisition---to configuration-level gate signals that quantify uncertainty at
the level of individual atomistic configurations. Coupled with \acs{MD} sampling
of the same chemical system, such configuration-level \acs{UQ} would improve
robustness by flagging specific critical snapshots along a trajectory for
\acs{DFT} labeling, rather than only flagging entire chemistries. We also plan to
extend the loop to multi-fidelity active learning where semi-empirical
tight-binding (\ac{DFTB}+) is used as an intermediate oracle between \acs{MLIP}
and full \acs{DFT}.

A further direction extends the framework from materials \emph{characterization}
toward materials \emph{discovery and design}. Because the present workflow is
exercised to establish the chemical and dynamical stability of candidate
structures, the natural next step is to support the actual discovery and design
of functional materials by driving atomistic simulations dedicated to the
collection of functional properties. This entails expanding the set of supported
computational tools beyond first-principles methods to include classical Monte
Carlo for thermodynamic properties and molecular dynamics for the retrieval of
mechanical behavior, so that the same agentic orchestration layer can progress
from certifying structural stability to predicting the functional and mechanical
response that ultimately governs materials selection.
