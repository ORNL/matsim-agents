\title{matsim-agents: An Agentic Artificial Intelligence Scientific Workflow for Autonomous
Atomistic Materials Discovery on Leadership-Class High-Performance Computing}

\author{

\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Oak Ridge National Laboratory}\\
Oak Ridge, TN, USA\\
lulopasim@ornl.gov}

\begin{abstract}
We present \textsc{matsim-agents}, an agentic \ac{AI} workflow framework for
atomistic materials discovery on leadership-class \ac{HPC} systems. The framework connects natural-language scientific objectives to
executable simulation tasks, \ac{MLIP}-based relaxation, uncertainty-aware
validation, selective first-principles escalation, and scientific summarization.
Its key novelty is a closed-loop, \acs{HPC}-deployable workflow in which uncertainty
estimates act as control variables that route predictions either to analysis or
to \ac{DFT}-driven active learning. The framework supports user-selectable \ac{MLIP} backends---HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} for
exascale-pretrained graph-neural-network potentials and UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}
for universal foundation-model potentials---and user-selectable \ac{DFT} backends,
including open-source \ac{QE} and licensed closed-source \ac{VASP}, with deployment
support for heterogeneous \ac{DOE} leadership-class systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Accelerating the discovery of novel functional materials remains one of the
grand challenges at the intersection of computational science and
\acs{HPC}. First-principles electronic-structure methods such
as \acs{DFT} provide accurate energies and forces but scale as $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ in
the number of electrons and are therefore prohibitively expensive for
high-throughput exploration of large chemical spaces. \Acp{MLIP}~\cite{behler2007,bartok2010,batzner2022} offer
near-\acs{DFT} accuracy at a fraction of the cost, but their reliability degrades
silently outside the training distribution, making undetected extrapolation a
practical risk in autonomous workflows.

Agentic \acs{AI} offers a principled route through autonomous planning, tool invocation,
conditional branching, and iterative self-improvement to address both challenges. By embedding an \ac{MLIP} inside a decision-making
loop equipped with \ac{UQ} and \acs{DFT} escalation, an
agentic workflow can \emph{explore broadly} with the \acs{MLIP} and \emph{correct
selectively} with \acs{DFT}, reserving expensive quantum-mechanical calculations for
only those configurations where the \acs{MLIP} is genuinely uncertain.

Existing active learning workflows for \acp{MLIP}~\cite{smith2018,vandermause2020,
podryabinkin2017} already automate the cycle of candidate generation, \acs{DFT}
labeling, and model retraining. \textsc{matsim-agents} advances this paradigm
by introducing a closed-loop agentic orchestration layer that connects
natural-language scientific objectives to executable atomistic simulation tasks,
\acs{MLIP}-based exploration, uncertainty-aware validation, selective \acs{DFT} escalation,
active learning, and language-level scientific synthesis. This loop transforms
materials simulation from a manually configured sequence of computational steps
into an autonomous scientific workflow in which planning, execution,
validation, correction, and reporting are coordinated by interacting agents.

The novelty of \textsc{matsim-agents} lies in making the full decision cycle
explicit and executable on leadership-class \acs{HPC} systems. A user can state an
open-ended materials objective in natural language; the planner converts that
objective into typed atomistic tasks; the executor performs fast HydraGNN-based
relaxation; the \acs{UQ} gate decides whether the prediction is reliable enough for
analysis or must be escalated to \ac{DFT}-driven active learning; and the analyst
returns an interpretable scientific summary. In this design, uncertainty is not
an offline diagnostic but a first-class workflow control variable that determines
whether the agentic system accepts, corrects, or improves its own predictions.

A second important capability is explicit user control over the first-principles
backend. The workflow supports both \ac{QE}~\cite{giannozzi2009qe,giannozzi2017qe}, an open-source \acs{DFT}
code, and \ac{VASP}~\cite{kresse1993vasp,kresse1996vasp}, a licensed closed-source \acs{DFT} code. The user can select the \acs{DFT}
engine most appropriate for their scientific problem, institutional software
access, pseudopotential library, and production workflow. To make this choice
practical on \acs{DOE} systems, \textsc{matsim-agents} provides detailed bash scripts
for compiling both \acs{QE} and \acs{VASP} on the three main \ac{DOE} leadership-class
supercomputing facilities: \ac{NERSC} Perlmutter, \ac{ALCF} Aurora, and \ac{OLCF} Frontier.

In this paper we present \textsc{matsim-agents}, an open-source framework that
realises this agentic materials-discovery loop. The main novel contributions of
this work are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{closed-loop agentic workflow} that translates natural-language
  materials objectives into executable atomistic simulation tasks through a
  checkpointed LangGraph multi-agent state machine, integrating \ac{LLM} planning,
  HydraGNN-based \acs{MLIP} relaxation, uncertainty-gated validation, \acs{DFT} labeling,
  active learning, and scientific summarization in a single reproducible loop.
\item A \textbf{\acs{DFT} warm-start capability} in which \acs{MLIP}-relaxed structures are
  used to initialize higher-fidelity first-principles optimization, benchmarked
  across a 2$\times$2 matrix of \acs{MLIP} backends (HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} and
  UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}) and \acs{DFT} solvers (\acs{QE} and \acs{VASP}). Beyond
  accelerating \acs{DFT} convergence, the cold-start vs.\ warm-start comparison serves
  two additional roles in the agentic workflow: it acts as a computational
  validation safety net for the \acs{MLIP} predictions by testing whether the
  \acs{MLIP}-relaxed geometry is genuinely closer to the \acs{DFT} minimum, and it
  exposes regions of \acs{MLIP} weakness in materials space---cases where warm-start
  benefit is absent or where \acs{DFT} convergence degrades---that can be used to
  target active learning data collection and improve model robustness.
\item An \textbf{\acs{HPC}-oriented deployment model} that supports local \acs{LLM}
  inference, \acs{GPU}-accelerated atomistic simulation, batch-scheduled
  first-principles calculations, checkpointed workflow execution, and
  platform-specific support for heterogeneous \ac{DOE} leadership-class systems.
\end{itemize}

The paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related
work. Section~\ref{sec:contributions} aggregates the distinctive contributions
that position \textsc{matsim-agents} relative to this literature.
Section~\ref{sec:architecture} describes the system architecture.
Section~\ref{sec:hpc} details the \acs{HPC} deployment strategy.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents benchmark results on Perlmutter.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} discusses findings, limitations, and future
developments, and draws conclusions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Machine-Learned Interatomic Potentials}
The past decade has seen rapid development of \acp{MLIP}, from kernel-based
Gaussian approximation potentials~\cite{bartok2010} and behler-Parrinello
networks~\cite{behler2007} to graph-neural-network architectures such as
NequIP~\cite{batzner2022}, MACE~\cite{batatia2022}, and
SchNet~\cite{schutt2018}. Foundation models trained on diverse datasets
(such as CHGNet~\cite{deng2023}, MACE-MP~\cite{batatia2023macemp}, and
UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}) have further broadened coverage. HydraGNN is an atomistic \ac{GFM} framework that has recently been used
in an exascale multi-task pretraining workflow over 16 open first-principles
datasets, spanning more than 544 million structures and over 85 elements, with
shared message-passing representations and dataset-/fidelity-specific
prediction heads~\cite{hydragnn,lupopasini2026exascalegfm}.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification for MLIPs}
\ac{UQ} for \acp{MLIP} has been studied via deep ensembles~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017},
\ac{MC}-Dropout~\cite{gal2016}, and calibrated committee models~\cite{schran2020}.
Ensembles are the most reliable but require $N$ independent model evaluations.
In multi-branch atomistic models, branch-selection signals can also expose
information about how predictions are routed across dataset- or
fidelity-specialized heads. In the HydraGNN \ac{GFM}, the BranchWeightMLP is a
composition-conditioned router that maps a structure's chemical-composition
vector to softmax weights over 16 dataset-/fidelity-specialized output
branches~\cite{lupopasini2026exascalegfm}. Such branch weights should be
interpreted as routing signals rather than calibrated Bayesian uncertainty.

\subsection{Active Learning for Materials}
Active learning has been used to iteratively expand \acs{MLIP} training sets with
targeted \acs{DFT} calculations, and several mature frameworks now automate this
cycle. FLARE~\cite{vandermause2020,vandermause2022} uses Bayesian force fields
with on-the-fly uncertainty to trigger \acs{DFT} calls during \acs{MD}.
DP-GEN~\cite{zhang2020dpgen} implements a concurrent-learning scheme for
DeePMD potentials that alternates exploration, labeling, and training across
many chemical systems. FIRETASKS / Atomate~\cite{mathew2017atomate} provides
a workflow engine for high-throughput \acs{DFT} and can be combined with \acs{MLIP}
training loops. ATAT and ICET~\cite{icet2019} focus on cluster expansion
fitting with active selection of supercell configurations.
ALF~\cite{schran2020} demonstrated committee-based active learning for
aqueous systems. More recently, foundation-model-aware AL schemes such as
Finetuner~\cite{batatia2023macemp} fine-tune MACE-MP on targeted datasets.
These frameworks demonstrate that candidate generation, uncertainty-based
selection, first-principles labeling, and retraining can be automated into
iterative materials-discovery pipelines.

\subsection{LLM-Driven Scientific Workflows}
Recent work has explored \acp{LLM} for materials hypothesis generation~\cite{
merchant2023,xie2023,sprueill2023}, automated literature synthesis, and code
generation. These developments show the potential of language models to assist
scientific reasoning, tool use, and human-facing interpretation of complex
simulation outputs.

\subsection{DFT Warm-Starting}
Using a fast approximate model to generate a near-equilibrium input geometry
for a more expensive method is a classical idea in computational
chemistry~\cite{lejaeghere2016}. Several recent works have proposed \acs{MLIP}
warm-starts for \acs{DFT} geometry optimisation~\cite{schreiner2022,merchant2023}.
These efforts motivate the use of learned potentials as fast preprocessors for
higher-fidelity first-principles calculations.

\section{Distinctive Contributions of \textsc{matsim-agents}}
\label{sec:contributions}

The related work reviewed above has established powerful components for modern
computational materials science, including graph-neural-network interatomic
potentials, uncertainty estimation, active learning, first-principles workflow
engines, \acs{MLIP}-assisted warm-starting, and emerging \acs{LLM}-based
scientific assistants. The distinctive contribution of \textsc{matsim-agents}
is to integrate these capabilities into a closed-loop, \acs{HPC}-deployable
agentic workflow for atomistic materials discovery. In this workflow,
natural-language scientific intent is translated into executable simulation
tasks, \acs{MLIP} predictions are evaluated through uncertainty-aware control
logic, selected structures are escalated to \acs{DFT} when higher fidelity is
required, and the resulting scientific evidence is returned to the user through
language-level synthesis.

The first distinguishing feature is the translation of open-ended materials
objectives into typed atomistic tasks. Instead of requiring the user to manually
assemble a sequence of scripts, configuration files, simulation inputs, and
post-processing steps, \textsc{matsim-agents} represents planning, execution,
validation, correction, and reporting as explicit states in a checkpointed
LangGraph multi-agent workflow.

The second distinguishing feature is the support for multiple \acs{MLIP} backends as
the fast atomistic execution engine. By default the workflow uses an
exascale-trained, multi-task HydraGNN checkpoint~\cite{hydragnn}: a compact
PaiNN-based \ac{GFM} built from 16 open first-principles datasets that provides both
high-throughput energy/force evaluation and branch-routing signals for low-cost
triage. A second supported backend is UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}, Meta FAIR's
family of universal foundation-model potentials trained on half a billion atomic
structures across molecules, materials, and catalysts. Both backends expose an
identical \ac{ASE} calculator interface, so the same workflow runs unmodified
regardless of which \acs{MLIP} is selected.

The third distinguishing feature is the use of the BranchWeightMLP as a
workflow-control signal. In the underlying HydraGNN model, BranchWeightMLP is a
composition-conditioned router that blends the predictions of 16 output
branches. In \textsc{matsim-agents}, the concentration or diffuseness of that
branch-weight distribution is used as a heuristic confidence signal: sharply
routed predictions can proceed to analysis, whereas diffuse routing can trigger
additional validation, \acs{DFT} labeling, or a more expensive \acs{UQ} method.
This interpretation preserves the technical meaning of BranchWeightMLP as a
router rather than overstating it as a calibrated epistemic uncertainty model.

The fourth distinguishing feature is the coupling of selective \acs{DFT}
escalation, active learning, and warm-starting inside the same agentic loop.
The framework can route uncertain structures to first-principles labeling,
append the resulting configurations to the training set, retrain the \acs{MLIP},
and use \acs{MLIP}-relaxed geometries as warm starts for higher-fidelity
geometry optimization. This connects fast approximate exploration with
selective quantum-mechanical correction.

The fifth distinguishing feature is the deployment model for leadership-class
\acs{HPC} systems. \textsc{matsim-agents} is designed around local \acs{LLM}
inference, \acs{GPU}-accelerated \acs{MLIP} evaluation, batch-scheduled
first-principles calculations, serialized workflow state, and platform-specific
support for heterogeneous \acs{DOE} supercomputers. This makes the framework a
scientific workflow system for production computational materials workloads,
not only an interactive tool-calling demonstration.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Overview}
\textsc{matsim-agents} is structured around three layers (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}):
(i) a \emph{natural language interface} layer accepting free-text objectives
or interactive chat; (ii) a \emph{LangGraph orchestration layer} implementing
the multi-agent graph; and (iii) an \emph{atomistic backend layer} providing
\acs{MLIP} relaxation, stability scoring, and \acs{DFT} execution. The remainder
of this section is organised around these layers. We first describe the
combined interface and orchestration layer, comprising the agent graph, its
nodes, and the three entry points that drive it; we then describe the atomistic
backend layer, comprising the HydraGNN \acs{MLIP} engine, the BranchWeightMLP
confidence signal, the active learning loop, and the \acs{DFT} warm-start
protocol.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/matsim-agents.png}
\caption{High-level architecture of \textsc{matsim-agents}. The LangGraph
orchestration layer routes between \acs{MLIP}-driven discovery and \acs{DFT}-driven active
learning based on the \acs{UQ} gate decision.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Interface and Orchestration Layer}
The first two layers of Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}---the natural-language interface
and the LangGraph orchestration layer---are realized together by a single
multi-agent graph and the entry points that expose it to the user.

\subsubsection{Agent Graph and Nodes}
The agent graph is assembled with LangGraph~\cite{langgraph} and contains four
primary nodes:

\textbf{Planner.} A configurable \ac{LLM} (Qwen2.5-72B served via
vLLM~\cite{vllm} by default, but interchangeable with any of the supported
open- or closed-weight models discussed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusions})
receives the user objective and emits a typed list of
\texttt{TaskSpec} items (\texttt{relax}, \texttt{analyze}, \texttt{report}).
Structured output via Pydantic schemas is used to ensure deterministic \acs{JSON}
parsing.

\textbf{Executor.} For each pending \texttt{relax} task, the executor invokes
the \acs{MLIP} relaxation tool, which runs \ac{ASE} \acs{FIRE}/\acs{BFGS}
optimisation using a user-selected \acs{MLIP} backend---either
HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} (graph-neural-network \acs{GFM}) or
UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} (universal foundation-model potential)---as a
force-and-energy evaluator. Results
(optimised structure, final energy, max force, convergence flag, \acs{UQ} scores) are
appended to the shared typed state.

\textbf{\acs{UQ} Gate.} After all tasks are executed, the gate evaluates the
branch-weight distribution for each relaxed structure. If the mean top-branch
weight exceeds a configurable threshold (default 0.6) and the fraction of
unreliable predictions is below a second threshold (default 0.25), the gate
passes control to the analyst. Otherwise, it triggers the active learning
handoff, logging the decision to a \ac{JSONL} audit file.

\textbf{Analyst.} The analyst node invokes the \acs{LLM} to generate a natural
language summary of the discovered structures, including energy rankings,
stability estimates, and \acs{UQ} signals.

\subsubsection{Entry Points and Task Complexity}
\textsc{matsim-agents} exposes these nodes through three entry points that form
a ladder of increasing task complexity. The simplest is the \emph{default
linear graph} described above: a fixed planner~$\rightarrow$~executor~$\rightarrow$~\acs{UQ}
gate~$\rightarrow$~analyst pipeline that handles a single, well-posed objective
(for example, ``relax this structure and report its stability'') in one
forward pass, with the only branch being the binary \acs{UQ} decision to escalate
or summarize. This entry point is best suited to one-shot tasks over a small,
explicitly given set of structures.

The second entry point is the \emph{supervisor graph}, which targets more
complex objectives that require systematic exploration of a composition space
rather than a fixed structure list. It decomposes the workflow into five
explicitly supervised nodes (prepare, explore, evaluate\_uq, optionally active
learning, and summarize) and adds a supervisor that can iterate over many
candidate phases, loop back into active learning when the \acs{UQ} gate flags
unreliable regions, and aggregate results across the enumeration. The added
node granularity gives the orchestrator finer control over how exploration,
validation, and correction are interleaved for multi-structure campaigns.

The third and most open-ended entry point is \emph{discovery chat}, an
interactive \ac{REPL} for exploratory, human-in-the-loop investigation. On top
of the underlying graph it adds automatic composition detection from
free-text input, optional multi-\acs{LLM} peer review (a proposer/critic debate
that stress-tests candidate hypotheses), and slash commands (\texttt{/relax},
\texttt{/al}, \texttt{/clear}) that let the user invoke individual stages on
demand. This entry point trades the bounded, single-objective scope of the
other two for open-ended, iterative scientific dialogue in which the task is
refined interactively rather than fixed in advance.

\subsection{Atomistic Backend Layer}
The third layer executes the physics. It evaluates energies and forces with the
HydraGNN \acs{MLIP}, derives a low-cost confidence signal from the model's
composition-conditioned branch router, and escalates uncertain structures to
\acs{DFT} through the active learning and warm-start protocols.

\subsubsection{HydraGNN GFM as the MLIP Engine}
HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn,lupopasini2026exascalegfm} is used in
\textsc{matsim-agents} as the fast atomistic execution engine. The checkpoint
used by the workflow is not a narrow, single-dataset force field; it is an
exascale-trained atomistic \ac{GFM} built from a multi-source,
multi-fidelity first-principles corpus. The pretraining campaign described
in~\cite{lupopasini2026exascalegfm} jointly trains on 16 open datasets
covering organic molecules, inorganic crystals, alloy surfaces, catalysts,
hybrid systems, and polymers, totaling more than 544 million structures across
more than 85 elements. This broad coverage is important for
\textsc{matsim-agents} because the planner can generate tasks involving
chemistries that were not manually enumerated in advance.

Architecturally, the HydraGNN \acs{GFM} follows a multi-task design: shared
\ac{MPNN} layers learn transferable atomistic interaction features, while
branch-specific output heads absorb dataset-dependent label conventions,
fidelity offsets, and partially labeled energy/force supervision. The exascale
pretraining study explored several \acs{MPNN} backbones through large-scale
\ac{HPO} on \acs{OLCF} Frontier, promoted the best candidates to fixed-budget
2,048-node training runs, and selected a compact PaiNN-based lead model. The
lead checkpoint contains approximately 12.1 million parameters, uses 16
output branches, and was trained in \acs{FP64}, making it suitable for both
high-throughput screening and integration into physics-based workflows.

In \textsc{matsim-agents}, this pretrained model evaluates energies and forces
for candidate atomistic structures during relaxation and screening. The
workflow therefore uses the HydraGNN \acs{GFM} as a practical \acs{MLIP}:
it provides orders-of-magnitude faster evaluations than direct \acs{DFT}, while
retaining a connection to the multi-domain training corpus through its branch
structure.

\subsubsection{BranchWeightMLP Confidence Signal}
The BranchWeightMLP was introduced in the HydraGNN \acs{GFM} to make the
multi-branch, multi-task architecture deployable on chemistries that may not
belong cleanly to a single training dataset~\cite{lupopasini2026exascalegfm}.
It maps a per-element chemical-composition descriptor to a softmax-normalized
weight vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$, with $\sum_k w_k = 1$,
that blends the predictions of the 16 output branches into a single energy and
force prediction. In the exascale pretraining study, this router contains about
24,500 parameters and was trained on 49 million samples distributed across the
training datasets, using energy and force errors from the branch-weighted
prediction as the training objective.

\textsc{matsim-agents} reuses the same branch-weight distribution as an inexpensive
workflow-level confidence signal. The top-branch weight
$w_\text{top} = \max_k w_k$ and the Shannon entropy
$H = -\sum_k w_k \log w_k$ indicate how concentrated or diffuse the router is:

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/BranchMLP_UQ.png}
\caption{BranchWeightMLP-based uncertainty heuristic in \textsc{matsim-agents}.
A candidate structure's chemical-composition vector is mapped by the
BranchWeightMLP router to softmax weights over the 16 dataset-/fidelity-specialized
HydraGNN output branches. The same weights both fuse the per-branch energy/force
predictions and yield routing-concentration statistics that
drive the \acs{UQ} gate: sharp routing is accepted for analysis, whereas diffuse
routing triggers additional validation or \acs{DFT} labeling.}
\label{fig:branchuq}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item $w_\text{top} \approx 1$ and $H \approx 0$ indicate that the chemistry is
  routed primarily through one dataset-/fidelity-specialized branch.
\item Lower $w_\text{top}$ and larger $H$ indicate that the model is blending
  several branches, which can occur for mixed or previously unseen chemistries.
\end{itemize}

BranchWeightMLP is a composition-conditioned branch router: it depends only on the chemical
composition of a system, so its routing weights are invariant to atomic
displacements and therefore constant along an \ac{MD} trajectory. The signal
thus distinguishes \emph{which systems (chemistries)} are routed diffusely, not
\emph{which configurations} within a fixed-composition trajectory are critical.
In \textsc{matsim-agents}, its entropy and top-weight statistics are therefore
used as a low-cost, system-level triage heuristic for agentic control: sharply
routed chemistries can proceed to analysis, whereas diffusely routed
chemistries can trigger additional validation or \acs{DFT} labeling
(Fig.~\ref{fig:branchuq}).

\subsubsection{Active Learning Loop}
When the \acs{UQ} gate triggers, \textsc{matsim-agents} executes a configurable
number of active learning iterations. Each iteration proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Candidate generation}: short \ac{MD} trajectories (\acs{ASE} Langevin or
  \ac{NVT}) are run from seed structures to sample configuration space; frames are
  collected as candidates.
\item \textbf{System-level uncertainty scoring}: because the BranchWeightMLP
  routing signal depends only on chemical composition, it is constant along a
  given trajectory and is used to flag which candidate \emph{systems}
  (compositions) are routed diffusely and therefore warrant escalation, rather
  than to rank individual frames within a trajectory.
\item \textbf{Within-trajectory snapshot selection}: for each flagged system,
  critical snapshots are drawn from its \ac{MD} frames using a greedy
  farthest-point diversity filter over configuration descriptors, which removes
  near-duplicate frames and retains geometrically distinct configurations.
\item \textbf{\acs{DFT} labeling}: the top-$n$ selected candidates are submitted to
  the user-selected \acs{DFT} backend, either \acs{QE} or \acs{VASP}, via
  batch job submission. \acs{QE} is open source, whereas \acs{VASP} is a licensed
  closed-source code that requires user-provided source access. Converged frames
  are converted to the extended-XYZ format.
\item \textbf{\acs{MLIP} retraining}: the new frames are appended to the training
  dataset and HydraGNN is retrained for a configurable number of epochs.
\item \textbf{Loop state serialization}: a \texttt{state.json} per iteration
  records candidate/selection counts, \acs{UQ} statistics, \acs{DFT} convergence rates,
  and timing. Fault-tolerant resume logic skips completed iterations.
\end{enumerate}

The loop is backend-agnostic: the \acs{DFT} backend interface accepts a
\texttt{DFTJobSpec} and returns a list of converged frames, allowing \acs{QE} and
\acs{VASP} to be used interchangeably from the workflow perspective. This design gives
users the option to rely on fully open-source \acs{QE} when software licensing and
reproducibility are priorities, or to use \acs{VASP} when a project requires an
established licensed production code and the user has access to the \acs{VASP} source
and pseudopotential files.

\subsubsection{DFT Warm-Start Protocol}
A separate but tightly integrated warm-start module is benchmarked across a
2$\times$2 combination of \acs{MLIP} backends and \acs{DFT} solvers:
\texttt{warmstart\_benchmark\_qe} targets \acs{QE} while
\texttt{warmstart\_benchmark\_vasp} targets \acs{VASP}, and each module accepts
either HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} (graph-neural-network \acs{GFM}) or
UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} (universal foundation-model potential) as the
pre-relaxation backend. This yields four benchmark configurations---HydraGNN+\acs{QE},
HydraGNN+\acs{VASP}, UMA+\acs{QE}, and UMA+\acs{VASP}---enabling direct comparison of
warm-start benefit across both \acs{MLIP} families and both \acs{DFT} solvers.

Beyond its primary role as a convergence accelerator, the cold-start
vs.\ warm-start comparison fulfills two broader functions within the agentic
workflow. First, it provides a computational validation safety net: if the
\acs{MLIP}-relaxed structure genuinely brings atoms closer to the \acs{DFT} energy
minimum, the warm run should require fewer \acs{BFGS} steps and fewer \acs{SCF}
iterations than the cold run; failure of this condition signals that the \acs{MLIP}
prediction may not be trustworthy for that chemistry. Second, systematic
collection of such failure cases across the benchmark fixture suite identifies
\emph{regions of \acs{MLIP} weakness} in materials space---compositions or
structural motifs where the model's pre-relaxation is unreliable---and this
information can be fed directly into the active learning loop to collect
targeted \acs{DFT} data and improve model robustness.

The benchmark pipeline, shared across all four configurations, proceeds as follows
for any input crystal structure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Run \acs{MLIP} \acs{FIRE} pre-relaxation to $f_\text{max} = 0.01$\,eV/\AA\ using
  either HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} or UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} (\acs{MLIP} warm start).
\item Run \acs{QE} \texttt{vc-relax} from the \emph{original} coordinates (cold
  start).
\item Run \acs{QE} \texttt{vc-relax} from the \emph{\acs{MLIP}-relaxed} coordinates
  (warm start).
\item Record and compare \acs{BFGS} step counts, per-step \acs{SCF} iteration counts,
  total \acs{SCF} iterations, final energies, and wall times.
\end{enumerate}

The same \acs{QE} input settings are used for both cold and warm runs to ensure a
fair comparison. The benchmark writes a structured \ac{JSON} summary alongside the
pw.x output files, enabling automated regression testing and reproducible
reporting.

\section{HPC Deployment}
\label{sec:hpc}

\subsection{Platform-Agnostic Design Principles}
The \textsc{matsim-agents} software stack is designed around three portability
principles:

\textbf{Module isolation.} All \acs{GPU}-software interactions (\acs{CUDA} version, \acs{MPI}
implementation, compiler toolchain) are encapsulated behind per-platform shell
fragments. A single function \texttt{load\_perlmutter\_modules\_gpu()} (or its
Frontier/Aurora equivalents) sets up the entire environment from a clean login
shell, making jobs bit-for-bit reproducible across re-submissions.

\textbf{Decoupled Python and \ac{MPI} environments.} HydraGNN inference requires a
PyTorch \acs{GPU} environment, while first-principles calculations require separate
\acs{MPI}/compiler stacks for \acs{QE} or \acs{VASP}. These are kept in separate shells: the batch
script activates the Python environment for the HydraGNN phase and spawns the
\acs{DFT} launcher as a subprocess that loads the appropriate compiler, \acs{MPI}, \acs{GPU}
runtime, and code-specific environment for the selected backend.

\textbf{vLLM inference serving.} \acs{LLM} inference is handled by a vLLM server
running in a separate Slurm allocation (or as a background process on a login
node for interactive sessions). The framework communicates with the server via
an OpenAI-compatible \ac{HTTP} endpoint, requiring only the \texttt{openai} Python
package on the client side.

\subsection{User-Selectable DFT Backends and Build Scripts}
\textsc{matsim-agents} exposes the first-principles code as a user-selectable
backend rather than hard-coding a single \acs{DFT} engine. The two supported options
are \acs{QE} and \acs{VASP}. \acs{QE} is open source and can be built,
modified, and redistributed according to its license. \acs{VASP} is a licensed
closed-source code; therefore, the framework can provide build and launch
scripts, but users must supply their own authorized \acs{VASP} source tree,
pseudopotential files, and institutional license access.

To make both options usable on leadership-class facilities, the repository
includes detailed bash scripts for compiling \acs{QE} and \acs{VASP} on \acs{NERSC} Perlmutter,
\acs{ALCF} Aurora, and \acs{OLCF} Frontier. These scripts document the module loads,
compiler choices, \acs{MPI} settings, \acs{GPU} offload configuration, and launcher
conventions required on each system. They are intended to reduce the amount of
site-specific trial-and-error normally required to deploy first-principles codes
inside an automated \acs{AI} workflow.

\subsection{Per-Platform Deployment}
The framework runs on all three \acs{DOE} leadership-class systems, each with a
distinct accelerator and \acs{QE} offload backend. On Perlmutter (NVIDIA A100),
\acs{QE} is built with NVHPC using \acs{CUDA} \ac{FFTW} offload and \ac{ELPA}
diagonalisation, and \acs{GPU}-aware \acs{MPI} is disabled for the single-node
warm-start benchmark to avoid \ac{GTL} startup overhead. On Frontier (AMD
MI250X), \acs{QE} uses OpenMP target offload through the HIP backend, with the
module fragment masking \texttt{ROCR\_VISIBLE\_DEVICES} to expose individual
\acp{GCD}. On Aurora (Intel \acs{PVC}), the \texttt{frameworks} module supplies
vLLM and \ac{XPU} PyTorch without a source build, \acs{QE} uses the OneAPI/OpenMP
offload backend, and a custom entrypoint wrapper works around a vLLM
model-registry SIGSEGV. In all cases the HydraGNN environment uses the
platform-native PyTorch build, and companion scripts provide the corresponding
\acs{VASP} path for users with licensed source access.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

All experiments in this section were run on \acs{NERSC} Perlmutter, one compute node
(4$\times$ NVIDIA A100 80\,GB), under Slurm \texttt{regular} QOS.

\subsection{UQ-Gated Relaxation Benchmark}
\label{sec:results:uq}
We evaluate the BranchWeightMLP \acs{UQ} estimator on three crystals spanning
different bonding regimes and structural complexity: Si in the diamond cubic
structure (8 atoms, covalent insulator), MgO in the rocksalt structure (8
atoms, ionic insulator), and MoNbTaW in a 2$\times$2$\times$2 BCC supercell

the expectation that HydraGNN's training distribution (MPtrj + ANI-1x)
covers simple binary oxides and elemental metals extensively. The result

the analyst without triggering an expensive active learning iteration.

All three \acs{MLIP} relaxations converged in a single \acs{FIRE} step. As discussed in

suggesting the BCC reference geometry already approximates the local energy
minimum well at the \acs{MLIP} level.

\subsection{Warm-Start Benchmark Setup}
\label{sec:results:warmstart_setup}
The warm-start benchmarks are organized as a controlled comparison between two
ways of initializing each first-principles geometry optimization:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cold start.} The \acs{DFT} optimizer is launched directly from the
  \emph{original} input coordinates of the fixture, with no \acs{MLIP}
  pre-processing. This is the conventional baseline.
\item \textbf{Warm start.} The same fixture is first relaxed by an \acs{MLIP}
  to $f_\text{max} = 0.01$\,eV/\AA, and the \acs{DFT} optimizer is then launched
  from the \emph{\acs{MLIP}-relaxed} coordinates. The \acs{DFT} settings are held
  identical to the cold run so that any difference in convergence is
  attributable solely to the change of initial geometry.
\end{itemize}

The cold/warm pair is replicated across a 2$\times$2 matrix of backends:
HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn} and UMA~\cite{wood2025uma} as the \acs{MLIP}
pre-relaxation engines, crossed with \acs{QE} and \acs{VASP} as the \acs{DFT}
solvers. This yields four configurations---HydraGNN+\acs{QE}, HydraGNN+\acs{VASP},
UMA+\acs{QE}, and UMA+\acs{VASP}---each of which is run in both cold and warm
modes. Crossing the two \acs{MLIP} families with the two \acs{DFT} solvers
separates the contribution of the pre-relaxation potential from that of the
first-principles optimizer, so that a warm-start benefit (or its absence) can be
attributed to the \acs{MLIP}, to the \acs{DFT} code, or to their interaction.

Three alloy fixtures provide the off-equilibrium ``hard cases'' that make the
cold/warm distinction meaningful: MoNbTaW \acs{HEA} (16-atom BCC supercell),
NbTaW BCC, and NbMoW BCC. A Si diamond fixture is included as a high-symmetry
reference case that establishes baseline correctness of the pipeline before the
alloy benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:results:warmstart_si}). For every
fixture$\times$backend combination, both the cold and warm runs are repeated
three times on Perlmutter to assess run-to-run reproducibility, and the
following quantities are recorded and compared between the cold and warm runs:
the number of \acs{BFGS} ionic steps, the per-step and total \acs{SCF} iteration
counts, the final total energy, and the wall time.

As described in Section~\ref{sec:architecture}, this cold-start vs.\ warm-start
comparison serves two roles beyond raw convergence acceleration: it is a
computational validation safety net for the \acs{MLIP} (a genuine geometry
improvement must reduce \acs{BFGS} steps and \acs{SCF} iterations), and it is a
probe for regions of \acs{MLIP} weakness (fixtures where warm-start benefit is
absent or where \acs{DFT} convergence degrades) whose failure cases can be routed
back into the active learning loop.

\subsection{Reference Case: Si Diamond}
\label{sec:results:warmstart_si}
The Si diamond fixture is the high-symmetry reference point of the matrix: it
validates that the cold and warm pipelines are wired correctly and produce

\section{Conclusions and Future Developments}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\subsection{Symmetry and the Warm-Start Benefit}
The Si diamond benchmark illuminates an important subtlety for warm-start
workflows: \emph{atomic forces alone do not capture all structural degrees of
freedom}. For high-symmetry crystals, ionic forces are exactly zero by symmetry
at \emph{any} lattice parameter, and the entire relaxation problem reduces to
optimising the unit-cell volume (or shape). \acs{FIRE}, which is an ionic optimizer,
will always converge in one step for such structures regardless of how far
the cell is from equilibrium. The warm-start benefit therefore vanishes for
high-symmetry elemental and binary systems.

The benefit reappears for: (1) low-symmetry structures where internal
coordinates are genuinely off-equilibrium (HEAs, substitutionally disordered
alloys, defect supercells, MOFs); (2) structures undergoing symmetry-breaking
phase transitions; and (3) molecular crystals with flexible organic linkers.
Fixture selection for warm-start benchmarks should prioritise these categories.

\subsection{Scalability and Cost}
On Perlmutter, the \acs{UQ} benchmark for three structures costs $\approx$14\,min
of \acs{GPU} time (one A100 node), dominated by HydraGNN model loading ($\sim$60\,s)
rather than inference ($<$1\,s per structure). Amortised over a production
discovery run of hundreds of structures, model loading cost is negligible.
The \acs{QE} warm-start benchmark for Si costs $\approx$6\,min including both \acs{DFT}
runs; the MoNbTaW benchmark (16 atoms, metallic, vc-relax) is expected to
require $\approx$20--40\,min per \acs{DFT} run at standard convergence thresholds.

\subsection{LLM Provider Flexibility}
A deliberate design decision is provider-agnosticism: the framework supports
Ollama, vLLM, OpenAI, Anthropic, and HuggingFace transformers backends.
On leadership-class machines without internet access, vLLM with locally
downloaded open-weight models is the standard deployment. The models directory
currently provides ten checkpoints spanning several independent developers, a
wide parameter range, and both dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures
(Table~\ref{tab:llms}). This selection lets the planning and analysis agents be
matched to the available accelerator memory and the desired quality/cost
tradeoff, rather than being tied to a single vendor or model.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Developments}
Current limitations include: (1) the active learning loop targets HydraGNN
retraining and does not yet support fine-tuning of universal \acsp{MLIP}
(UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}, MACE-MP~\cite{batatia2023macemp}); the warm-start
module, by contrast, already supports both HydraGNN and UMA as pre-relaxation
backends; (2) the warm-start protocol handles ionic coordinates but not cell
shape/volume, which limits benefit for pure-strain relaxation problems; (3) the
\acs{LLM} planner does not yet incorporate literature search or database queries
(Materials Project, AFLOW) as tool calls; and (4) the present \acs{UQ} gate relies
on the BranchWeightMLP routing heuristic, which is composition-conditioned and
therefore constant across atomistic configurations of the same chemical system.
Future work will address these limitations, including the integration of more
robust \acs{UQ}, for example deep ensembles of independently trained \acsp{MLIP}
or \ac{MC}-Dropout, that quantifies uncertainty at the level of individual
atomistic configurations. Coupled with \acs{MD} sampling of the same chemical
system, such configuration-level \acs{UQ} would improve robustness by flagging
specific critical snapshots along a trajectory for \acs{DFT} labeling, rather than
only flagging entire chemistries. We also plan to extend the loop to
multi-fidelity active learning where semi-empirical tight-binding (\ac{DFTB}+) is
used as an intermediate oracle between \acs{MLIP} and full \acs{DFT}.

\subsection{Summary}
We have presented \textsc{matsim-agents}, an open-source agentic \acs{AI} scientific
workflow framework whose central novelty is the closed-loop orchestration of
natural-language scientific intent, \acs{MLIP}-based atomistic execution,
uncertainty-aware validation, selective \acs{DFT} escalation, active learning, and
language-level scientific synthesis on leadership-class \acs{HPC} systems. The system integrates \ac{LLM} planning, \acs{MLIP} relaxation (HydraGNN~\cite{hydragnn}
and UMA~\cite{wood2025uma}), \acs{UQ}-gated decision making, active learning,
first-principles escalation, and \acs{DFT} warm-starting into a unified, portable
pipeline designed for \acs{NERSC} Perlmutter, \acs{OLCF} Frontier, and \acs{ALCF} Aurora. It
addresses all five subtopics of the ``Scientific Workflows \& Application
Development'' theme of the AgenticAI4HPC workshop: intelligent workflow
composition through a checkpointed LangGraph multi-agent graph; iterative
optimisation through a self-improving active learning loop; agent-assisted
validation through the \acs{UQ} gate; \acs{AI}-driven algorithm design through the
branch-weight uncertainty mechanism; and tight integration of simulation (\acs{DFT}),
data analytics (\acs{MLIP} inference), and \acs{AI} (\acs{LLM} planning) workloads.
Benchmark results on Perlmutter confirm correct \acs{UQ} behaviour for representative
crystal classes and validate the warm-start benchmarking infrastructure for
genuinely off-equilibrium structures such as the MoNbTaW \acs{HEA}, positioning
\textsc{matsim-agents} as a practical framework for agentic \acs{AI} workflows in
demanding computational materials science on leadership-class \acs{HPC}.
